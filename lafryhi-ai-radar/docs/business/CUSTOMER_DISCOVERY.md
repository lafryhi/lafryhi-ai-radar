# Customer Discovery Protocol

**Version:** 1.0

## Research Goals

- Discover the most painful AI-related decision problem.
- Understand the alternatives customers use today.
- Measure the urgency and frequency of the problem.
- Identify what customers perceive as valuable.
- Understand evidence, transparency, and trust requirements.
- Discover willingness to pay and what outcome would justify payment.

Customer discovery should investigate past behavior and specific decisions. It should not attempt to persuade the participant or validate the interviewer's preferred idea.

## Participant Context

Record only the context needed for analysis:

- Customer segment and role.
- Business type and approximate size.
- Current AI adoption level.
- Relevant decision frequency.
- Acquisition source.
- Whether the participant reached a Decision Brief.

Protect confidential information and avoid collecting sensitive data that is not necessary for the research purpose.

## Interview Questions

Use these as a flexible guide rather than a script. Follow concrete examples.

1. How do you currently follow AI developments relevant to your work?
2. Tell us about the last AI-related business decision you made.
3. What triggered that decision?
4. What made the decision difficult?
5. Which alternatives did you consider?
6. Which sources did you trust, and why?
7. How much time did the research and decision take?
8. Who else, if anyone, was involved in making or approving it?
9. What happened after the decision?
10. How did you know whether the decision worked?
11. When do you decide to ignore an AI development?
12. What would make a Decision Brief trustworthy to you?
13. What information would you need before acting on a recommendation?
14. What would make you return to a product like this every week?
15. What result, if any, would make this product worth paying for?

Avoid leading questions such as “How helpful was our recommendation?” when usefulness has not been demonstrated. Prefer “What, if anything, did you do differently after reading the Brief?”

## Interview Practice

1. Ask for permission to take notes.
2. Begin with the participant's existing workflow.
3. Ask about the most recent real example.
4. Separate what happened from what the participant believes might happen.
5. Probe for time, cost, risk, frequency, and consequences.
6. Show the product only after understanding the existing problem when possible.
7. End by asking whether another person faces the same decision problem.
8. Write a brief evidence summary immediately after the conversation.

## Feedback Classification

| Classification | Meaning | Example follow-up |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Bug | The product behaves incorrectly | Reproduce conditions and impact. |
| UX friction | The intended path is difficult | Identify exact step and observed behavior. |
| Confusion | The message or concept is misunderstood | Ask the user to explain it in their own words. |
| Missing value | The experience works but does not improve a decision | Identify the expected outcome and current alternative. |
| Feature request | A proposed capability | Investigate the underlying job and frequency. |
| Trust concern | Evidence, privacy, confidence, or recommendation concern | Identify what proof or control is missing. |
| Pricing objection | Price or payment model prevents purchase | Separate affordability, value, timing, and trust. |
| Performance issue | Speed or reliability reduces usability | Record context, duration, and abandonment. |
| Positive outcome | A useful result occurred | Capture the decision, action, and observable benefit. |
| Retention signal | The user returns or intends to repeat a real workflow | Record trigger, frequency, and repeated behavior. |

## Evidence Levels

| Level | Strength | Appropriate use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| One user opinion | Weak signal | Log it; investigate the underlying job. |
| Repeated pattern | Multiple independent users report the same problem | Consider a focused experiment. |
| Behavioral evidence | Observed usage confirms the problem or value | Prioritize measured funnel or workflow changes. |
| Revenue evidence | A genuine customer pays or renews because of value | Strong evidence of willingness to pay. |
| Retention evidence | Users repeatedly return and receive value | Strong evidence of an enduring need. |

A feature request from one user is not sufficient evidence for a major feature. The team should look for the underlying job, repeated patterns, observed behavior, retention, or revenue before committing substantial development.
